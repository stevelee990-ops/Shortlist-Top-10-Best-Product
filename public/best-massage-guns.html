How to choose

Check amplitude and stall force for deep tissue

Amplitude is how far the head travels. Tom's Guide recommends 14–16mm for deep-tissue or sports massage (the Theragun Prime, D6 Pro and Theragun Pro have 16mm), while minis like the Bob and Brad Q2 Mini (7mm) feel gentler. Stall force is how hard you can press before the motor stops: Garage Gym Reviews rates 30–50 lbs as decent for a full-size gun and 60 lbs or more as top tier. TechGearLab notes more power isn't always more comfortable.

Don't buy a gun that's too slow

Tom's Guide suggests avoiding guns that top out below about 1,400 percussions per minute (PPM) unless you only want a light massage. Most picks here run between roughly 1,700 and 3,200 PPM.

Mind the weight

You'll hold a massage gun in one hand, and experts quoted by NBC Select warn that a heavy one can tire your arm before you finish. Minis like the Ekrin Athletics Bantam weigh about 1 lb; full-size guns range from about 1.5 lbs (Hypervolt Go 2) to nearly 3 lbs (Bob and Brad D6 Pro).

Pick the right heads and battery

Tom's Guide suggests at least three attachments and 60 minutes of battery. A large round head suits big muscles such as quads, a flat head suits dense areas like shoulder blades (NBC Select), and a soft cushion head is kinder to sensitive spots (TechGearLab). USB-C charging is the most convenient.

Treat apps as a bonus

Apps from Therabody and Hyperice offer guided routines, but Tom's Guide says apps, cases and Bluetooth are extras that shouldn't outrank speed, amplitude and comfort.

For travel, think small

The Hypervolt Go 2 is TSA-approved for carry-on bags, per Garage Gym Reviews, and the Bantam and Q2 Mini come with hard cases. A case that holds every attachment keeps heads from getting lost.

Questions people ask

Are massage guns worth it?

Yes, for many people: experts cited by Garage Gym Reviews say regular use can help reduce muscle soreness, improve circulation and increase flexibility, and a study cited by Tom's Guide found short-term gains in range of motion. The research is still limited, so a mid-priced gun like the Hypervolt Go 2 or a sub-$100 mini is a sensible way to try one.

How long should you use a massage gun?

About 10 to 30 seconds per spot to start, and no more than two minutes on any one muscle group per session, according to UCLA Health. HSS suggests light 10- to 15-second passes, and Garage Gym Reviews says a full session rarely needs more than 10 to 15 minutes.

Can you use a massage gun every day?

Yes, you can use a massage gun daily, and even several times a day, according to UCLA Health, as long as you start on a low speed and keep the time on each muscle short. Overworking one spot can irritate the muscle or cause bruising, HSS warns.

Where should you not use a massage gun?

Keep a massage gun on muscles and away from bones, joints and nerves, and don't use it on fresh strains, sprains, bruises or open wounds, according to HSS and Garage Gym Reviews. People with fractures, deep vein thrombosis or active inflammation should avoid them, and anyone pregnant, with circulation problems, osteoporosis or diabetes, or on medication should check with a doctor first.

Is Theragun or Hypervolt better?

Neither is better for everyone: Theraguns such as the Theragun Prime offer deeper 16mm strokes and a triangle grip, while Hypervolts such as the Hypervolt Go 2 are lighter, quieter and cheaper. Garage Gym Reviews calls the Go 2 more than enough for most people, and picks a Theragun if you want a stronger massage and app routines.

Should you use a massage gun before or after a workout?

Both work: HSS suggests brief 10-second passes on tight spots after an active warm-up, and longer use after hard sessions as a cool-down. Tom's Guide notes a study advising against using one right before strength, balance or explosive activities, since it could reduce performance.
